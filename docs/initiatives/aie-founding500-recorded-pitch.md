## The script

### 1 · Open — who I am (0:00, ~45s)

Hi — I'm Matthew Hopkinson, founder of AstraJax.

One thing about me before I sell you anything: I have never handwritten a line of code. Not one. Everything I've shipped — production software, used by real teams, at real scale — I've built with AI, on top of clean data. I'm a commercial operator, not an engineer. And that's not a disclaimer. It's the whole point. I'm the living proof of the thing I'm about to pitch you.

So let me tell you what AstraJax is, why it exists, and why I think it's the part of this whole AI wave that most people are quietly getting wrong.

### 2 · The bottleneck (~0:45, ~1.5 min)

Here's the bottleneck.

Everyone can see how powerful these tools are getting. The pace is ridiculous. Everyone wants in — everyone wants the leverage. But the frontier is still, overwhelmingly, built by developers, for developers. And even the friendly no-code tools carry the same assumptions, the same language, the same hidden terminal window. The technical tribe still owns the door.

So what actually happens inside a real company? Someone buys the tool. They get a brilliant demo. It works once, in a clean example. Then real life arrives — messy data, nobody owns the output, the team's nervous, the prompts are vague — and the whole thing quietly dies in week two. People blame the model. They go back to the old way.

The market has solved *building* agents. It has not solved *using* them. That gap — trust, context, governance, and the human story underneath — that is the unsolved problem.

### 3 · The moat is adoption (~2:15, ~45s)

So here's the line I'd bet the company on: **the moat is adoption.**

Not another agent builder. Not another demo that sparkles once and dies in week two. The hard part of AI was never really the technology. It's the data, the workflows, and the humans. And almost nobody is building for the humans.

### 4 · What AstraJax is (~3:00, ~1 min)

AstraJax is **the AI command centre for operators.**

It takes the person who knows the work best — the commercial leader, the ops expert, the founder — and puts them in the architect's seat. Not "go away and learn to think like a developer." The system adapts to *them*: their context, their judgement, their pace. They build the business brain. They shape the agents. And they stay in charge of what "good" means.

The belief underneath it is simple: **domain experts don't need to become technical. With AI, they can become architects.**

Built by an operator, for operators.

### 5 · Proof — the Butternut story (~4:00, ~3.5 min)

Now, I'm not theorising. Let me show you where this comes from.

In my last role I was Director of Sales at Butternut Box — a UK pet food company. I was employee number 33; it grew into a confirmed unicorn. I built and governed the operating layer for our Direct Sales channel: roughly £8.1 million of annual spend, across three P&Ls, with a 120-person commercial team running about 1,500 events a year.

And our tools could not keep up. We ran that entire operation on Gmail, WhatsApp, Notion and Google Sheets. No CRM. No finance software. Brilliant people, doing genuinely high-value work, with embarrassingly low-leverage tools.

So I did the boring thing first. For about twelve months — and this is the part everyone skips — I cleaned the data and rebuilt the workflows. No agents. No magic. Just turning chaos into a real operating system. It was deeply unglamorous. It was also the single most important thing I did.

Because once that foundation existed, the exciting layer moved fast. We launched our first agent fleet — fourteen agents — in about three weeks. And that speed was *only* possible because the boring layer was already there. If you put agents on top of messy data, you don't get intelligence. You get very confident chaos machines. Fun. Useless.

And here's *how* those agents worked — this is the pattern I actually care about. Take our heaviest job: organiser emails. Hundreds of them, every one different. An email comes in. AI reads it and pulls out what matters. One agent links it to the right event. A second agent *proposes* the updates — and, crucially, it never writes to the live record. A human sees the before-and-after and approves. Only then does a third agent execute the change and write the audit trail.

One job per agent. One clear handoff. Clean data. A human approval gate. A paper trail on everything. That is the whole design philosophy: bounded agents that propose, humans who decide, audit throughout.

And the payoff wasn't just speed. Better travel planning saved roughly £180,000 a year. At scale, the system handed back around 3,000 hours a year of capacity. And because the data was finally clean, it started changing *decisions* — we could see that our employed salespeople outperformed in the right conditions, and that underpinned a major shift in how we staffed the whole channel. The old system could tell us what happened. The new one helped us decide what to do next.

### 6 · The cast — why adoption is the moat (~7:30, ~1.5 min)

But the bit I'm proudest of isn't the plumbing. It's that people actually *used* it.

We gave the agents personalities. There's Clive — a needy Victorian gentleman whose job is to teach the team how to use the system. There's Reggie, who runs the fiddly bonus admin nobody enjoys. And that's not decoration — it's an adoption strategy. Bots people are scared of don't get used. Bots people can laugh at, argue with, and accuse of flirting with the logistics manager absolutely do. One of my team said, in public, about a chatbot: "I think I fancy him." Our debug bot once issued a public apology in Slack for Clive's behaviour. The agents had developed office politics.

That sounds silly. It's the most serious thing in the system. Because the moment people start *playing*, they start learning — and adoption stops being a battle.

That body of work is what put me on Airtable's flagship stage at Airspace LA, got me named an Airtable MVP, and is why I'm here in the HyperAgent Founding 500. Airtable's own recap described it as building fourteen agents in three weeks — with zero engineering background.

### 7 · The product now (~9:00, ~1.5 min)

So AstraJax productises exactly that pattern.

It's a guided loop. First it maps the *operator* — not the business, the person — so it knows where to teach and where to get out of the way. Then it builds the business brain: the context, the rules, what "good" looks like — all human-approved before anything is trusted. Then the operator shapes the fleet: scoped agents, clear jobs, approval rules.

And it runs on a cast you can actually remember. **Clive reasons** with you. **Pam challenges** — she's the raised eyebrow, the one who stress-tests your thinking before you act. **The Architect** — the human — **decides.** And **Doc executes** the approved work out to the runtime.

Propose, challenge, human gate, execute. We call it the Trinity. It's simple enough for a non-technical team to hold in their heads — which is the only kind of process that actually gets used.

### 8 · Shadow AI (~10:30, ~45s)

And yes — someone always asks: isn't this just shadow AI? Good. Let's have that fight.

Shadow AI is *already* happening. Your people are already pasting things into tools you never approved. You don't beat that by banning curiosity — you beat it by giving curiosity rails. **If you make AI too hard to use safely, people will use it unsafely.** AstraJax is the governed command centre that brings it out of the shadows: clean context, approval gates, cost discipline, audit trails. Decentralised — not uncontrolled.

### 9 · HyperAgent, who it's for, offers (~11:15, ~1.5 min)

On the runtime — this is where HyperAgent comes in. **HyperAgent makes powerful agents possible. AstraJax makes them adoptable** by the teams who know the work. We sit on top, we stay portable, and the client's approved context brain is theirs to keep.

Who's it for? Commercial and operations-heavy teams who want AI their people will actually use — where one or two operators hold too much in their heads, and where the last AI experiment stalled the moment the demo ended.

And we meet them where they are: a short **Adoption Readiness Audit** to find where it's stalling; a **Brain and Fleet Sprint** to build the first one *with* them; or a deeper partnership when they want to move fast.

### 10 · Close (~12:45, ~30s)

So that's AstraJax.

The market solved building agents. We're solving the part that actually decides whether *any* of this works — adoption. The moat is adoption.

The AI command centre for operators. Built by an operator, for operators.

**AstraJax. AI that actually gets used.**

Thank you.
